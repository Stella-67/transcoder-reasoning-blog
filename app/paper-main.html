1 Introduction

Language models learn solution strategies from data and encode them in largely opaque, high-dimensional computations, making it difficult to determine how their capabilities are implemented. Mechanistic interpretability addresses this problem by explaining model outputs in terms of internal computation. Existing approaches operate at several levels: residual-stream and attention analyses trace information through architectural components [8]; ablation, activation patching, and targeted editing test whether components are causally necessary [6, 18, 21]; and dictionary-learning methods decompose dense activations into sparse features that can be described in human terms [5, 23]. Transcoders are particularly useful for circuit analysis because they reconstruct a component’s output from its input, turning an MLP computation into a set of interpretable feature units [7].

Recent work combines cross-layer transcoders (CLTs) with attribution graphs to describe model computation as prompt-specific circuits of sparse features. Ameisen et al. [1] train CLTs to approximate transformer MLP computations and use them to construct an interpretable local replacement model. Attribution graphs derived from this model represent active features as nodes and direct linear attributions between them as edges. Their methodology also includes graph pruning and visualization for interpreting these circuits, together with perturbation experiments that test graph-derived interactions and evaluate the replacement model’s mechanistic faithfulness. Lindsey et al. [15] apply this framework to Claude 3.5 Haiku across behaviors including multi-step reasoning, planning, multilingual processing, and hallucination.

Our main contributions are as follows:

  • We develop a parameter-efficient latent-mixing CLT for Gemma-3-4B-IT [11], providing a sparse feature basis for circuit analysis.
  • We construct attribution graphs for factual and mathematical reasoning and assess their use for candidate discovery. The graphs surface meaningful features, while Neumann scores do not directly recover measured single-feature effects.
  • We characterize L22:F31850 as a sentence-boundary continuation axis that strongly changes reasoning layout, while its effect on AIME accuracy remains modest in aggregate and heterogeneous across problems.

2 Preliminaries: Feature-Level Circuit Discovery

2.1 Motivation: From Residual Streams to Cross-Layer Features

Residual streams analysis confounded by superposition Before a circuit can be drawn, we must decide what its nodes are. The transformer’s own architecture is the obvious place to look. In the standard residual-stream account [8], every attention and MLP sublayer reads from a shared residual state and writes an additive update that all later sublayers can read. This is a precise map of where information can move, but it is silent on what is computed in interpretable terms. And for that question the architectural units fail at both grains. Attention heads and MLP blocks are too coarse to be semantic units; individual neurons are too entangled, since features live in superposition rather than one-per-neuron [5, 23]. A single residual-stream direction mixes several concepts, and the nonlinear map an MLP computes does not decompose into readable feature-to-feature edges. The units we want are not in the architecture; they have to be learned.

Transcoders supply computational units. Learning the units is exactly what Anthropic’s transcoder method does: it keeps the transformer’s residual stream, attention, and normalization, but re-expresses each MLP through a learned sparse feature model [1]. Which sparse model to learn is not a free choice. A sparse autoencoder reconstructs an activation vector, so it answers a representational question: what is present in a residual state. A transcoder instead reconstructs the output of a model component from that component’s input [7], so it answers a computational question: how to decompose the computation in the MLP into interpretable units. The computational question is the one circuit analysis asks. Concretely, a transcoder rewrites the MLP’s nonlinear input–output map as sparse feature activations followed by decoder writes; once attention patterns, normalization denominators, and feature masks are frozen for a prompt, the replacement is locally linear and direct feature-to-feature effects are well defined [1]. The resulting division of labor runs through the whole paper: the residual stream determines where an intervention can propagate, and the transcoder features supply the units that can be named, ranked, and causally edited.

Cross-layer transcoders removes per-layer fragmentation. One obstacle remains. A plain transcoder gives each layer its own private dictionary, so a concept the model carries through many layers reappears as a different, formally unrelated feature at every depth. A cross-layer transcoder removes this fragmentation: a CLT feature reads from the residual stream at its source layer and may write into the reconstructed MLP outputs of that layer and all later ones [1]. A feature thereby becomes a node in a cross-layer computation rather than a layer-local proxy, and an attribution path can run through the one feature whose activation and downstream writes explain the computation, rather than through a chain of per-layer stand-ins. In this paper the feature basis is trained for Gemma-3-4B-IT, a 34-layer, width-2560 decoder-only transformer [11], with M = 100,800 features per layer and a structural top-k sparsity cap of 500 active features per token and layer.

2.2 Overview: Candidate Feature Discovery and Intervention with CLTs

The CLT lets us score each feature’s contribution to a prediction. With a CLT in place, we can build an attribution graph for a chosen prompt and target logit. Its nodes are the active CLT features, the input tokens, and the output logits, and its edges measure how strongly each node influences another. Following the local-replacement construction of Ameisen et al. [1], the transformer’s nonlinear quantities—attention patterns, normalization denominators, and the sparse feature masks—are frozen at the values they take on the prompt, so that feature-to-feature effects can be read off by backpropagating through the resulting linear graph. We use this same machinery to recover readable circuits [1, 15].

Attribution proposes; intervention verifies. Where this paper is deliberately conservative is in how much such a graph is allowed to claim. An edge weight, or a Neumann path-sum aggregating many edges, is an attribution score inside a local linearization; it need not equal the change one would measure by actually intervening on the feature in the full nonlinear model. We therefore treat the attribution graph only as a hypothesis generator and let intervention adjudicate. From this point on, the behavior under study is fixed: at a sentence-ending period in an AIME-style reasoning trace, Gemma-3-4B-IT can stop, emit a newline, or carry the argument forward with a logical connective such as We, However, Since, or So, and we look for the feature-level circuits that incentivize the connective continuation. Concretely, the study proceeds in three steps: (i) we train a CLT for Gemma-3-4B-IT and build an attribution graph toward such a connective logit; (ii) we read off the handful of features it ranks highest as candidates for that behavior; and (iii) we put the leading candidate through explicit perturbation experiments—a fixed-prompt linear diagnostic that isolates its direct token-level effect, followed by autoregressive AIME generation that measures its behavioral consequences—keeping a causal claim only where the intervention supports it. This carries down to individual features the discipline the broader circuit-discovery literature applies to candidate subgraphs, which are validated by ablation, patched execution, or masked-model fidelity rather than by saliency alone [3, 6, 21, 29].

Roadmap. The rest of the paper instantiates this pipeline. Section 3 defines the CLT architecture and training objective used to reconstruct Gemma-3-4B-IT’s MLP outputs. Section 4 constructs prompt-specific attribution graphs and explains how Neumann scores are turned into the displayed graphs. Section 5 selects L22:F31850 from recurrent attribution candidates and validates it by direct perturbation, first in a fixed-prompt linear diagnostic and then during autoregressive AIME generation. The case study that survives this pipeline is L22:F31850: attribution surfaces it in However-continuation contexts, but intervention shows that its causal role is broader, namely suppressing newline/stop behavior at period positions and promoting continued mathematical prose.

The MLP sublayers are our focus. Substantial evidence indicates that they store much of a transformer’s factual and conceptual knowledge: they behave as key–value memories whose value vectors promote concrete output tokens [12], and localized edits to mid-layer MLPs can rewrite specific factual associations [18]. Yet they are difficult to interpret neuron by neuron. Individual hidden neurons are polysemantic, participating in many unrelated behaviors, which is what one expects when a layer represents more features than it has neurons by placing them in superposition [5, 9].

3.2 From Transcoders to Cross-Layer Transcoders

Transcoders. Per-layer dictionaries do not capture cross-layer structure. The plain transcoder of Equation (1) learns an independent feature dictionary at each layer. However, information written to the residual stream can persist and be read or modified by later layers. Separate dictionaries may therefore encode the same underlying feature repeatedly at different depths, producing attribution graphs with duplicate nodes connected by mostly uninformative identity-like edges [14]. Such graphs make it difficult to distinguish the persistence of an existing feature from the creation of a new computation.

Cross-layer transcoders. A cross-layer transcoder (CLT) [1] addresses this problem by allowing each feature to model effects across multiple layers. Rather than reconstructing only the MLP output at its source layer, a feature encoded at layer s can decode into the MLP outputs of layer s and all later layers. Its downstream influence can therefore be represented by one feature node instead of being rediscovered separately at each layer, yielding a more compact attribution graph.

Implementation. Training pairs are extracted with forward hooks on the frozen Gemma-3-4B-IT’s MLPs: each unmasked token contributes the residual input xℓ,t and MLP output mℓ,t across all layers [24, 26]. Feature activations are sparsified by a JumpReLU with threshold 𝜃 = 0.025, followed by a global top-k gate that keeps the k = 500 largest features per token and layer across the tensor-parallel shards. We train the analyzed 100,800-feature latent-mixing CLT with AdamW (constant learning rate 10−4, weight decay 10−2, PyTorch-default betas, seed 42) at B = 64 tokens per step, on 8 NVIDIA A100 80 GB GPUs with one rank generating these activations and seven training (1.96 steps per second). The reconstruction loss falls from 0.911 at initialization to 0.0064 by step 50,000 (Figure 2); with the loss converged, we stop training there, after about 7.1 hours.

3.5 Why the Training Is Effective

A CLT is only useful as a hypothesis generator if it is a faithful and interpretable surrogate for the MLP computation it replaces. We assess this along three intrinsic axes: reconstruction fidelity, sparsity, and feature interpretability.

Reconstruction generalizes across evaluation distributions. We evaluate the trained CLT on held-out StackExchange, AIME 2024–2025, NaturalReasoning, Medical-o1, and WikiQA. Across these five distributions, reconstruction MSE ranges from 0.0059 to 0.0083, and pooled relative L2 error from 0.29 to 0.36 (Table 2). The consistency of these errors indicates that reconstruction transfers beyond StackExchange-style training data. Reconstruction quality varies with depth: the relative L2 error ∥hℓ −ĥℓ∥2∕∥hℓ∥2 is inverted-U-shaped across layers, rising from 0.15 at layer 0 to 0.67 at layer 13 before falling to 0.28 at layer 33 (Figure 3). This peak coincides with a trough in the activation norm itself, so the middle layers are the thinnest rather than the worst reconstructed in absolute terms. The same profile appears across datasets, indicating a consistent weakness of the surrogate at middle layers rather than dataset-specific degradation.

Activations are sparse. On the held-out set, a token activates only 58.6 of the 100,800 features per layer on average, a density of 0.06% (Figure 4). Most layers are even sparser: the median layer activates 10.4 features, and 24 of the 34 layers stay below 20 active features per token. The mean is pulled upward by the deepest layers, where the count rises toward the top-k cap of 500. Thus each MLP output is reconstructed from a small, enumerable set of active features rather than a dense mixture of the full dictionary, making downstream attribution graphs tractable.

Taken together, our reconstruction error and activation sparsity are comparable to those reported for Anthropic’s production-scale CLTs [1].

Features are interpretable. Faithful, sparse reconstruction supports circuit analysis only if the individual feature directions are themselves interpretable. We run the CLT over a random sample of 20,000 OpenWebMath [19] passages and, for every one of the 34 ×100,800 ≈3.4M features, record its firing rate together with the ten passages that most strongly activate it and the token position of each peak. Selection then follows from statistics, not manual taste: we discard the first-token artifact, retain those with a non-trivial peak (maximum activation at least 4 in our normalized activation units), and label each retained feature by automated interpretability [4], prompting a language model to describe what its maximally-activating examples share.

Table 3 reports six retained features chosen to span the dictionary’s depth (layers 0 through 31) and the kinds of structure it learns: early-layer lexical concepts, mid-layer formatting and reasoning features, and deep-layer register features. Across the three top passages shown for each feature, the automated labels remain coherent.

4 Attribution as Candidate Discovery

To make the goal of this section concrete, consider the prompt

“… it is easy to show that m forms a basis.”

which Gemma-3-4B-IT continues with the token However. In this case the model could have stopped, started a new line, or kept explaining in some other way; instead it produced a discourse connective that carries the argument across the sentence boundary. To find the feature-level circuit behind that choice, we build an attribution graph toward the However logit.

High-level workflow. Given a prompt and target logit, we first construct a prompt-specific graph of direct influences among input tokens, CLT features, and the output. A truncated Neumann series then aggregates paths through intermediate features into total influence scores. These scores determine which feature nodes are displayed, while the direct-effect edges among the displayed nodes form the compact attribution graph.

4.1 Direct Effect Computation

The first step toward the attribution graph is computing its edges: the direct effect of one node on another, i.e. how strongly a source node (a feature or an input token) pushes on a target node’s readout (a downstream feature’s pre-activation, or an output logit) in a single step, before any routing through intermediate features.

We compute these direct effects in a locally linearized replacement model: every MLP sublayer is replaced by the trained CLT, while the remaining nonlinearities (attention probabilities, RMSNorm scale factors, and the JumpReLU/TopK active-feature masks) are frozen at their forward-pass values and gradients are not allowed to propagate through them. We write gi→j for the direct effect from source node i to target node j, computed as the corresponding gradient in this frozen model. The gradient is also blocked from passing through any other CLT feature’s encode–decode chain, so influence routed through a third feature is excluded. This procedure yields a direct-effect value for each admissible source–target pair.

4.3 From Neumann Scores to Attribution Graphs

To make the scores easier to inspect, we visualize them as a compact attribution graph. For a fixed prompt and target continuation logit, equation (10) gives a total influence score from every token or feature source to that logit. We retain the 100 CLT features with the largest positive Neumann scores toward the target logit and assemble them into a directed graph. The direct-effect matrix G supplies the edges among these nodes, while the Neumann score determines how strongly each source is attributed to the target after accounting for paths through intermediate features.

4.4 A Worked Example: From an Attribution Graph to a Partial Causal Trace

To make the graph-to-explanation workflow in Section 4.3 concrete, we study the factual completion The capital of France is → Paris. Graph attribution nominates influential features and links, controlled activation tests their provisional semantic roles, and intervention tests a candidate path through them. Our goal is to recover one supported circuit fragment, not an exhaustive or unique account of the computation.

Graph-based candidate selection and activation specificity. From the prompt-specific attribution graph, we retain CLT features at the literal capital, literal France, and final is positions whose aggregated Neumann attribution exceeds 0.01, yielding 1,034 candidates. Controlled changes in wording, entities, and relations then test whether their activation patterns match a proposed role in the trace France/capital → answer state → Paris.

Figure 8 reports representative activation evidence for the two input features retained after graph attribution, activation screening, and ablation: L0:F26582 at France and L0:F81670 at capital. Appendix A gives the complete prompt suite and selection procedure.

France token. The screen identifies three layer-0 candidates. Among them, targeted ablations identify L0:F26582 as the clearest individual contributor to the Paris output, while joint ablation indicates distributed input support. Across 24 France sentences, 24 other-country controls, and 120 unrelated sentences, France is its maximally activating token in 23∕24 France contexts, and sentence maxima separate from unrelated text with AUC 0.972 (Figure 8(a)). Its weaker responses to unrelated text motivate the narrower description literal-France feature, rather than France-only detector.

Capital token. Among six high-attribution candidates tested against seven relation controls, L0:F81670 ranks second and third by direct and multi-hop Paris attribution among 155 features, and its ablation lowers P(Paris) by 0.0446. Across 24 capital-reference, 28 alternative-relation, and 120 unrelated sentences, it peaks at capital in every capital context (Figure 8(b)), although some unrelated words activate it more strongly. L0:F20862 is more activation-selective but does not support the Paris logit, so we retain L0:F81670 as the literal-capital input feature.

Partial shared mediator. We next search for a shared downstream feature that receives positive graph edges from both retained inputs, responds to their ablation, and positively supports Paris. The strongest candidate is L33:F66785 at the final is position (Figure 9).

Figure 9(b) shows that the proposed edges are strong relative to their local backgrounds. The France and capital edges rank at the 97.46th and 94.03rd percentiles among their sources’ positive outgoing edges, and at the 98.29th and 99.43rd percentiles among positive edges entering the mediator.

5 Selecting and Validating the Target Feature

This section looks for the CLT feature that actually drives the behavior introduced at the start of Section 4: after a sentence-ending period in mathematical reasoning, the model continues with a logical connective instead of stopping or starting a new line.

Feature Discovery. Feature discovery runs Neumann propagation on six sentences drawn from AIME reasoning traces. Each sentence ends where Gemma-3-4B-IT assigns substantial probability to a logical-connective continuation ( However, Since, and So). For each sentence we rank features by Neumann score toward its continuation logit and keep the top 100.

We compare the top-100 lists across all six sentences, identifying each feature by its (layer, feature) pair and dropping token position. This yields five recurrent candidate features. Each appears in the top 100 for at least five of the six sentences.

Recurrence serves as an initial consistency criterion, but selection for detailed study additionally requires semantic interpretability. Each candidate has an automated-interpretability label from Section 3.5, computed on training-corpus top activations and independent of the six sentences. Table 4 reports these labels together with representative maximally activating OpenWebMath examples.

Under this protocol the response scales linearly with the edit, but Neumann scores do not predict causal magnitude. None of the observed-to-predicted ratios approaches one, and two Neumann-positive features have negative measured slopes.

This discrepancy is not a nonlinearity artifact: it persists even in the frozen linear regime. The mismatch is structural: the Neumann score credits f with the entire multi-hop path sum toward the logit, while the measured slope perturbs f alone. When negative routes dominate at the perturbed position, the marginal effect can point opposite to the aggregate score. Attribution scores are rankings, not causal magnitudes, and must be checked by intervention.

Combining the semantic and intervention evidence, we select L22:F31850 for the subsequent generation-time study. Its automated-interpretability label and top-activating examples align most directly with algebraic proof and logical continuation in AIME reasoning. It also has the largest observed-to-predicted ratio among the five candidates, indicating that the greatest fraction of its Neumann-predicted effect is realized under intervention. The selection therefore reflects both semantic relevance and causal support.

6 Perturbation Experiments

This section investigates what role the selected feature plays when Gemma-3-4B-IT generates AIME solutions autoregressively. We use the same scale-factor edit defined above in equation (11), but apply it during generation.

6.1 Setup

Period-triggered perturbations. L22:F31850 activates particularly strongly at sentence-ending period positions, so we restrict the intervention to those positions. Across the six discovery sentences, L22:F31850’s mean activation on sentence-ending period tokens is 3.1–8.6× higher than on other tokens, with a pooled ratio of 5.3×; Figure 10 illustrates this concentration for two representative sentences. This consistent selectivity provides a natural trigger: during autoregressive generation, every token whose decoded form ends in a period is treated as a candidate intervention position, and the edit is applied when L22:F31850 has positive baseline activation at that position. The resulting protocol focuses the intervention on positions at which the feature is most consistently active.

Cache-propagated period interventions. At each period trigger, the runner pauses generation and performs three full-prefix forwards. A clean vanilla forward records the target-feature activation and clean logits, caches the attention softmax patterns and RMSNorm scaling factors, and stores the per-layer MLP outputs used to compute reconstruction errors. A zero-perturbation replacement forward then computes the baseline CLT activations, fixes the JumpReLU/Top-K firing masks, and computes the reconstruction errors that are held fixed during the edit. An edited replacement forward applies the scale-factor edit to L22:F31850 while reusing these cached nonlinear states and captures the resulting K and V vectors for the period position. These perturbed K/V entries are written back into the live autoregressive cache, allowing the intervention to influence subsequent token generation through both attention weights and value aggregation [27].

Implementation. We evaluate on the 30 problems of AIME 2024 and the 30 of AIME 2025 [16], with problems and reference answers taken from public datasets [2, 17]. For each problem and setting, we sample 128 solutions at temperature 1.0 with a 2,048-token generation budget under three settings: the unedited model (Vanilla), amplification (sf=3), and reversal (sf=−1).

A solution is scored correct if its final boxed integer equals the reference answer. The edited runs contain 344,124 (sf=3) and 319,946 (sf=−1) period firings. Comparisons across settings are restricted to the 7,141 problem–sample pairs for which all three settings produced a solution.

6.2 Results

We evaluate the cache-propagated intervention at three levels of granularity: the next-token distributions recorded at each intervened period (Figures 11 and 12), the surface organization of complete sampled solutions (Tables 6 and 7), and final-answer accuracy (Figure 13). Across all three levels the same picture emerges: scaling L22:F31850 acts as a bidirectional control over the stop-versus-continue decision at sentence boundaries, and the resulting accuracy effect is real but problem-dependent.

The perturbation shifts next-token logits. Figure 11 reports the paired change in next-token probability at each intervened period. Because the clean and edited distributions are evaluated on the same prefix, this comparison isolates the immediate effect of the current intervention.

Amplification reduces the combined probability of \n and \n\n by 29.8 percentage points, from 54.9% to 25.0%. The largest increases occur for Since (+14.4 points) and We (+10.6), followed by smaller gains for So, Let, In, and However. Reversal shifts the distribution in the opposite direction: the combined newline probability rises by 10.3 points, from 75.0% to 85.3%, while Since and We decrease by 1.8 and 3.0 points. The effect is not a uniform increase in sentence-initial connectives, since Then, Also, and Thus all decrease under amplification. Instead, the perturbation redistributes probability between newline tokens and a restricted set of continuations led by Since and We.

Empirical continuation frequencies reflect the probability shifts. Figure 12 reports the empirical frequencies of tokens generated after sentence-ending periods under each setting, complementing the fixed-prefix probability changes in Figure 11. The three settings have comparable numbers of period continuations, permitting direct comparison of their normalized compositions. Amplification reduces the newline share from 65.8% to 24.4%; separately, Since rises from 2.8% to 18.3%, We from 4.6% to 17.1%, and the pooled frequency of So, Let, In, and However from 4.0% to 14.8%. Reversal raises the newline share to 83.1%, while Since and We fall independently to 0.1% and 0.5%, and the pooled connective group falls to 0.4%. Thus, the fixed-prefix probability shifts survive sampling and are not tied to a single lexical item: scaling L22:F31850 rebalances line breaks against a restricted family of sentence-initial continuations throughout the autoregressive trajectory.

The token-level shift compounds into the layout of entire solutions. Compounded over an entire generation, the token-level shift produces a large change in how solutions are laid out on the page. Over the 7,141 matched problem–sample pairs, amplification roughly halves the number of lines per solution (37.6 →20.0) and doubles the mean line length (40.5 →81.8 tokens), while reversal moves both statistics in the opposite direction (44.2 lines of 33.7 tokens each; Table 6). Table 7 makes this aggregate shift concrete in two matched examples. For AIME 2025 Problem 10, the unedited solution places each step of the case analysis on its own line, whereas amplification (sf = 3) joins each case into a continuous sentence chain that reaches the same roots. For AIME 2025 Problem 21, the corresponding contrast is one fact per line versus connected paragraphs. These examples are the per-solution manifestation of the line-count statistics in Table 6.

Accuracy effects are modest in aggregate and heterogeneous across problems. Figure 13 reports both absolute final-answer accuracy and the per-problem change relative to Vanilla for AIME 2024 and 2025, with all three settings scored on identical matched samples. Aggregate effects are modest. The aggregate, however, understates the per-problem structure, because more than half of the problems are at a 0% floor under every setting and the remaining problems move in both directions. Amplification more than doubles accuracy on 2024 Problem 12 (17.3% →41.3%) and adds 12.5 points on 2024 Problem 2 (70.3% →82.8%), yet costs 4.7 points on 2024 Problem 23, where reversal is instead the beneficial direction (18.8% → 27.3%). No single setting is best on every problem: the intervention reliably changes how the model writes, and whether that helps depends on the problem.

A representative trajectory-level effect. Figure 13 establishes that the accuracy effect is problem-dependent. Table 8 focuses on the clearest positive case, AIME 2024 Problem 12, where amplification raises accuracy from 17.3% to 41.3%. At the displayed period, the edit changes the top next-token prediction from a line break to Since; the sampled continuation then performs the hours-to-minutes conversion that a representative Vanilla failure omits. The clean/edit probability comparison is paired on an identical prefix within the amplified rollout. The Vanilla text comes from a separate rollout and is included as an illustrative failure, not as a common-prefix counterfactual completion.

7 Conclusion

The intervention isolates a direct behavioral role for L22:F31850: at sentence-ending periods, increasing its activation suppresses newline and stop behavior and promotes a restricted set of connective continuations, especially Since and We. Its effect on correctness, however, is conditional rather than uniformly beneficial. Continuing the argument helps when the unedited model breaks or concludes before a necessary step, as in AIME 2024 Problem 12, but can hurt when a problem benefits from committing to an answer or following a different trajectory. Consequently, a large and reproducible behavioral effect yields only a modest aggregate accuracy change and heterogeneous per-problem effects. The central distinction is therefore between controlling how the model continues its reasoning and improving whether it reaches the correct answer: L22:F31850 causally steers the former, but it is not a general-purpose accuracy knob.
